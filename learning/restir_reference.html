The problem: many lights, one shadow ray budget

Direct lighting at a shading point is an integral over every light in the scene. With a handful of lights you can just sample all of them. With hundreds, you can't — so you have to pick, and picking well is the entire problem ReSTIR exists to solve.

Direct lighting integralthe target quantity
\[ \class{mjxvar v-Ldirect}{L_{\text{direct}}}(\class{mjxvar v-p}{p}, \class{mjxvar v-wo}{\omega_o}) = \int_{\Omega} \class{mjxvar v-Le}{L_e}(\class{mjxvar v-wi}{\omega_i})\, \class{mjxvar v-fr}{f_r}(\class{mjxvar v-p}{p},\class{mjxvar v-wi}{\omega_i},\class{mjxvar v-wo}{\omega_o})\, \class{mjxvar v-cos}{\cos\theta_i} \; d\class{mjxvar v-wi}{\omega_i} \]

Sum the light arriving from every direction in the hemisphere \(\Omega\), weighted by the BRDF and the cosine term. With many small lights, almost all of \(\Omega\) contributes nothing — the integral is dominated by a few narrow solid angles you have to actually find.

Single-sample NEE estimator (uniform light pick)camera.h :: sample_direct_lighting()
\[ \class{mjxvar v-Ldirect}{L_{\text{direct}}} \approx \class{mjxvar v-numlights}{N_{\ell}} \cdot \frac{\class{mjxvar v-Le}{L_e}\, \class{mjxvar v-fr}{f_r}\, \class{mjxvar v-cos}{\cos\theta_i}}{\class{mjxvar v-pdfdir}{p_{\text{dir}}}} \]

Pick one of \(N_\ell\) lights uniformly, importance-sample a direction toward it, trace one shadow ray. Unbiased, and exactly what shipped in today's code — but noisy once \(N_\ell\) is large, because most picks are for a light that barely matters at this point.

Resampled importance sampling (RIS)

RIS is the trick that makes "many lights" affordable: score several cheap candidates against an ideal target distribution you can't sample directly, then keep one, weighted so the result is still unbiased.

Resampling weightplanned · reservoir.h
\[ \class{mjxvar v-wiw}{w_i} = \frac{\class{mjxvar v-phat}{\hat p}(\class{mjxvar v-xi2}{x_i})}{\class{mjxvar v-psrc}{p}(\class{mjxvar v-xi2}{x_i})} \]

\(x_i\) are candidates drawn cheaply from an easy distribution \(p\) (e.g. uniform over lights, same as today's NEE). \(\hat p\) is a cheap-to-evaluate stand-in for the true integrand — typically the unshadowed contribution \(L_e f_r \cos\theta\), no shadow ray yet. \(w_i\) scores each candidate by how much better \(\hat p\) thinks it is than \(p\) already assumed.

RIS estimatorplanned · reservoir.h
\[ \class{mjxvar v-Ldirect}{L_{\text{direct}}} \approx \class{mjxvar v-fy}{f(y)} \cdot \class{mjxvar v-Wy}{W_y}, \qquad \class{mjxvar v-Wy}{W_y} = \frac{1}{\class{mjxvar v-phat}{\hat p}(\class{mjxvar v-y}{y})}\left(\frac{1}{\class{mjxvar v-M}{M}}\sum_{i=1}^{\class{mjxvar v-M}{M}} \class{mjxvar v-wiw}{w_i}\right) \]

Pick one candidate \(y\) from the \(M\) candidates with probability proportional to \(w_i\) (weighted reservoir sampling, next section), then pay for exactly one true, expensive evaluation \(f(y)\) — the one that includes the shadow ray. \(W_y\) corrects for the biased way \(y\) was chosen, keeping the whole thing unbiased.

Weighted reservoir sampling (WRS)

The streaming algorithm that picks "one sample, weighted by \(w_i\)" out of a sequence without ever storing more than one candidate at a time — which is exactly what makes it cheap enough to merge across pixels and frames later.

Streaming update ruleplanned · reservoir.h :: update()
\[ \class{mjxvar v-wsum}{w_{\text{sum}}} \mathrel{+}= \class{mjxvar v-wiw}{w_i}, \qquad \class{mjxvar v-M}{M} \mathrel{+}= 1, \qquad \class{mjxvar v-y}{y} \leftarrow \begin{cases} \class{mjxvar v-xi2}{x_i} & \text{with prob. } \class{mjxvar v-wiw}{w_i} / \class{mjxvar v-wsum}{w_{\text{sum}}} \\ \class{mjxvar v-y}{y} & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \]

Process candidates one at a time, in any order, keeping only three numbers: the current pick \(y\), the running weight total \(w_{\text{sum}}\), and the count \(M\). After every candidate has streamed through, \(y\) is exactly distributed proportional to its weight among all of them — a provable result, and the reason this composes (a whole reservoir can be re-fed into another one as if it were a single candidate; see Spatial Reuse).

Final unbiased contribution weightplanned · reservoir.h
\[ \class{mjxvar v-Wy}{W_y} = \frac{\class{mjxvar v-wsum}{w_{\text{sum}}}}{\class{mjxvar v-M}{M} \cdot \class{mjxvar v-phat}{\hat p}(\class{mjxvar v-y}{y})} \]

Read straight off the reservoir's own bookkeeping once streaming is done — no separate pass over the candidates needed. Multiply by \(f(y)\) (the one true, shadow-ray-verified evaluation) to get the pixel's direct-lighting estimate.

Spatial reuse

The payoff step: after every pixel builds its own small reservoir (say \(M=4\) candidates), pull in a few neighboring pixels' already-chosen samples as if they were additional candidates. Neighboring pixels usually shade similar geometry, so a good pick for one is usually a good pick nearby too.

Combining a neighbor's reservoirplanned · reservoir.h :: combine()
\[ \class{mjxvar v-wcombine}{w} = \class{mjxvar v-phatcur}{\hat p_{\text{here}}}(\class{mjxvar v-yn}{y_n}) \cdot \class{mjxvar v-Wn}{W_n} \cdot \class{mjxvar v-Mn}{M_n} \]

Re-score the neighbor's chosen sample \(y_n\) under this pixel's own target function \(\hat p_{\text{here}}\) (different shading point, so a different \(\hat p\)) instead of trusting the neighbor's score as-is, then feed \((y_n, w)\) into this pixel's reservoir via the ordinary streaming update rule above. \(M\) accumulates too (\(M \mathrel{+}= M_n\)), so a reservoir that's absorbed several neighbors effectively remembers how many total candidates it represents.

Temporal reuse

Exactly the same combine operation as spatial reuse, but across frames instead of across pixels: reproject the current pixel into last frame's position and merge in its reservoir. This is the "T" in ReSTIR, and it's what lets a handful of fresh candidates per frame compound into an effective sample count in the hundreds.

M-capping (bounding temporal lag)planned · reservoir.h
\[ \class{mjxvar v-Mprev}{M_{\text{prev}}} \leftarrow \min\bigl(\class{mjxvar v-Mprev}{M_{\text{prev}}},\; \class{mjxvar v-kcap}{k} \cdot \class{mjxvar v-Mcur}{M_{\text{cur}}}\bigr) \]

Without this, a reservoir's \(M\) grows every frame forever, so last frame's sample eventually outweighs this frame's brand-new candidates by a huge margin — the image stops responding to a light turning on, an object moving, anything changing. Clamping \(M_{\text{prev}}\) to some multiple \(k\) (Bitterli et al. use around 20) of this frame's own candidate count bounds how much history one merge can carry, trading a little variance for responsiveness.

Bias & correction

Why naive reuse isn't quite unbiasedgood to know, not needed for a first pass
\[ \class{mjxvar v-phatcur}{\hat p_{\text{here}}} \neq \class{mjxvar v-phatn}{\hat p_{\text{neighbor}}} \]

Every shading point technically has its own target function \(\hat p\) (different BRDF, normal, distance to the light), and reusing a neighbor's sample without a geometric correction term (a Jacobian, since the sampling domain implicitly shifts) introduces a small systematic error. Bitterli et al. 2020 show the biased-but-practical version first, then derive an unbiased correction using a generalized multiple-importance-sampling weight across every reservoir being combined. The bias is usually small and visually acceptable for a first working version — worth knowing about, not worth building before spatial/temporal reuse even works.
