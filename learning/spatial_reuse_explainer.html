camera.h · spatial reuse

Where a pixel's extra light candidates actually come from

RIS already gets each pixel four scored candidates and one shadow ray. Spatial reuse hands a pixel a few more candidates for free — ones its neighbors already drew and paid for. Here's the mechanism, step by step, in the same shapes the code uses: trace_primary(), combine(), rescored().

00 The idea in one picture

Without spatial reuse, every pixel is an island: it draws its own light_candidates (4), keeps one, resolves it, done. With spatial reuse, a pixel also looks at a handful of already-built neighbor reservoirs before it resolves anything — each one is a free extra candidate, because that neighbor already did the expensive part.

01 Why this needed two passes, not one

The old render loop did everything for one pixel in one shot: trace, build a reservoir, resolve, move to the next pixel. That breaks the moment a pixel wants to borrow from a neighbor that hasn't been processed yet in the same sweep — there's nothing there to borrow.

So render() now runs two full-image passes per sample, with a hard barrier between them: every thread finishes phase A before any thread starts phase B.

02 Phase A: build, but don't resolve yet

For each pixel, phase A does exactly what plain RIS always did — draw light_candidates candidates, stream them through a reservoir — and then stops. No shadow ray. The winner sits there with its p_hat cached, waiting for phase B to decide whether it's the final answer or just one more candidate for someone else's merge.

03 Phase B: which neighbors are even allowed?

For each pixel, spatially_combined_reservoir() tries spatial_neighbors (4) random cells within spatial_radius (20) pixels. A neighbor only counts if its surface actually looks like this pixel's own — otherwise reusing its reservoir would mean, say, borrowing a floor pixel's chosen light for a wall pixel behind it. Two checks gate that:

dot(normal, neighbor.normal) < 0.9 catches a different surface orientation. |t_here − t_there| > 0.1·max(t) catches a different distance from the camera — same idea, different axis.

04 The catch: a direction only means something from where it was drawn

A neighbor's winning candidate was aimed as a direction from their point. Copying that direction and re-anchoring it at this pixel's point aims at nothing in particular — it doesn't even hit the light anymore. That's why light_sample stores the actual world-space light_point instead: a fixed spot in space, valid from anywhere.

05 combine(): one more candidate, not a special case

Once a neighbor's winner is re-scored at this pixel's point, it's just another candidate — it enters the same streaming reservoir the pixel's own four already went through, weighted by p_hat_here · other.W() · other.M so it correctly counts as standing in for all M candidates the neighbor already resampled down to one.

06 Resolve once — still just one shadow ray

However many candidates fed the merge — 4 of your own, or 20 across 5 neighbors — only the final winner ever gets traced against the scene. Spatial reuse changes how good that one shadow ray's target is; it never changes how many get fired.

07 What it actually bought, measured

On the 12-small-lights stress scene, against an 800-spp reference:

1.07×lower error at equal 16 spp budget vs. RIS alone
0.986error ratio at 128 spp — tracks RIS alone, doesn't plateau
1shadow ray per pixel per sample, unchanged

The convergence number is the important one: if the Jacobian correction this implementation deliberately skips (see reservoir.h::combine()) were introducing real bias, error would stall above the reference instead of continuing to close the gap. It doesn't — it tracks plain RIS almost exactly at high sample counts, which is the signature of a technique that's unbiased-in- practice at this reuse radius, not just fast.
